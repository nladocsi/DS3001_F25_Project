\begin{abstract}
The purpose of this project is to estimate the likelihood that an adult develops diabetes in the United States based on demographic and behavioral characteristics. Diabetes is one of the most prevalent chronic diseases in the U.S. and is associated with severe long-term health complications. As rates of obesity and physical inactivity rise, the ability to identify factors that lead to increased risk is critical for prevention and early intervention efforts. Through examining social, behavioral, and biological variables of interest, this project aims to better understand patterns of diabetes risk across the adult population. 

To address this question, we use data from the 2024 National Health Interview Survey (NHIS), which provides information on respondents' health, socioeconomic conditions, and lifestyle behaviors, making it well-suited for analyzing diabetes risk factors. In collecting their data, the NHIS gathers results from around the country. Therefore conclusions drawn reflect larger national trends and are not limited by sampling constraints.

After identifying key variables related to diabetes and its potential predictors, we constructed a unified diabetes indicator variable by combining three separate variables. We merged gestational diabetes, pre-diabetes, and diagnosed diabetes into a single outcome variable. This outcome variable would be used to indicate if a respondent had diabetes. This allowed both diagnosed and at-risk individuals to be captured in the outcome because pre-diabetes and gestational diabetes increases the likelihood of developing type 2 diabetes. 

In order to model diabetes risk, DecisionTreeClassifiers were used due to their ability to handle categorical variables and non-linear relationships. The dataset was split into an 80/20 train-test split that was used universally in all the models to ensure consistency and accuracy in the comparison of results. The two individual decision trees achieved similar accuracy values of around 0.78, but high true positive and low true negative rates indicating instability. Improvement of the model revealed that the optimal maximum tree depth value was 10. In order to address variance found in the confusion matrices of the individual trees, an ensemble of 1,000 trees were bootstrapped to improve stability and slightly increased the accuracy to 0.789. A random forest model reaffirmed these results and identified age and high cholesterol as the most influential predictors. Overall, the models were successful in identifying risk factors, but the precision of individual predictions was limited. 

\end{abstract}

\section{Data Description}
\label{submission}
With the aim of this project being to develop a model that classifies whether or not an adult has diabetes in the United States, the NHIS data was essential to understand. In-person household interviews are conducted and a variety of yes/no, and multiple-choice type questions are used to obtain data. The variables and data collection through this survey on demographic and behavioral characteristics are what we turned to when selecting significant variables. It was first necessary to classify which variables would allow for a determination on if our respondent actually had diabetes. The three variables used to identify a diabetes diagnosis are “Ever had diabetes,” “Ever had prediabetes,” “Ever had gestational diabetes”. For those three data Variables: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item GESDIB\_A: Ever had gestational diabetes with 1 denoting yes and 2 no
    \item PREDIB\_A: Ever had prediabetes with 1 denoting yes and 2 no
    \item DIBEV\_A: Ever had diabetes with 1 denoting yes and 2 no
\end{itemize}
A 1 in the data collection indicates a “Yes”, a 0 indicates a “No”, and 7-9 indicates either “Refused to answer”, “Don’t know”, or “Not ascertained”. For proper cleaning the three variables were combined into one new variable, DIAB\_ANY. Furthermore, we removed all values 7-9, replacing all with the nan value.
Now that our target variable is cleaned and manageable we turned to select our predictor variables. After looking through all the possible variables in the codebook we knew the initial predictor variables must be basic demographic identifiers. We selected age, sex, race, region of household: 
\begin{itemize}
    \item SEX\_A: Sex of sample adult, with 1 denoting a participant coded as male and 2 a participant as female
    \item RACELLP\_A: Race of participant
    \item REGION: Region participant's household is located in
\end{itemize}
Once again we combined 7-9 response options, replacing the values with nan. These factors are a good initial start for a diabetes prediction model. Furthermore, we wanted more specific characteristics for lifestyle habits and situational factors. The factors we chose were, closeness to poverty line, body mass index, education level of sample adults, general health status, frequency of leisurely walks.
\begin{itemize}
    \item RATCAT\_A: Ratio of family income to poverty threshold for patient
    \item EDUCP\_A: Educational level of patient
    \item PHSTAT\_A: General health status
    \item WLKLEIS\_A: If the patient walked for leisure in the past 7 days with 1 denoting yes and 2 no
\end{itemize}
Those four variables were again cleaned to have all the vague 7-9 responses combined and changed to be nan values. 
After a more broad but important look into the lifestyle of the responders through these variables we wanted to target some of the most influential factors that correlate to diabetes. With some basic background research into diabetes we found that cholesterol levels along with levels of hypertension are two of the most determining factors that could predict if an adult could suffer from diabetes in their future. With this in mind we finished off our predictive variable selection with “Ever had high cholesterol?”, “Have you ever been told you had hypertension?”, and the surveyor's BMI and age.  
\begin{itemize}
    \item BMICAT\_A: Categorical body mass index of patient
    \item HYPEV\_A: Ever been told they have hypertension with 1 denoting yes and 2 no
    \item CHLEV\_A: Ever had high cholesterol with 1 denoting yes and 2 no
    \item AGEP\_A: Age of patient
\end{itemize}
These were all continuous variables and therefore did not have to be recoded for vague responses as the other variables above did.
The final predictive factor that needed significant cleaning was regarding the food insecurity variables. We thought determining the connection diabetes had to food insecurity would provide significant information and a more accurate predictive model. Handling these separate variables similarly to the overall diabetes variable, we took 5 variables that all had relevance towards running out of food, worries of being hungry, or used food stamps. Combining these variables we were left with a new variable named, FOOD\_INS. The new code for this variable is “1” indicates food insecurity present, while “0” indicates no food insecurity. One last time the 7-9 response code was replaced with nan values. 

Overall cleaning for this dataset was very straightforward. With our chosen dataset most of the variables are categorical, but given a numerical key. This means the formatting of the binary number keys did not need adjustment because the respondent was not given the freedom to personalize their response. The final step before moving on to coding our model was creating a new table with only the relevant data. The NHIS data is extremely large and therefore we were unable to attach the full CSV to our github repository. By creating a new table with only the relevant data a more manageable file can be analyzed.

\subsection{Individual Decision Tree Models}

After initial visualization and exploratory analysis we choose to utilize a decision tree model to further examine our data. A decision tree model was chosen because it handles categorical features, when encoded numerically, extremely efficiently. Furthermore, there is no assumption of linearity that occurs with a decision tree model. While exploring the relationship between diabetes and different demographic characters it was unlikely that the variables affected risk linearly. Additionally, the interpretability of a decision tree model is high, meaning the logic is easily understood. The visualization of this model allows for easy analysis on which variables matter most, the variables closest to the top of the tree are often the most influential. Lastly, utilizing a decision tree model allows analysis into how different variables work together to influence the risk of diabetes. The variables are not all looked at in isolation, but instead the splits reflect combinations of characteristics. 
With that justification in mind we first fit two individual decision trees to predict diabetes status from demographic, health, and food-insecurity-related variables. The cleaned dataset was split into an 80/20 train-test partition and this train-test split was applied to all models and used consistently to ensure accurate comparison. All models were trained on the 80 percent training set and evaluated on the 20 percent test set. The 80/20 split rationale is a standard practice in model learning, but specifically for this data set it was a necessary protocol. Our NHIS dataset is significantly large so this split allows for adequate data for our training and evaluation. 80 percent being our training portion ensures the model has a significant amount of examples to determine the patterns across the different demographic and health characteristics. The 20 percent test set, also allows for statistically reliable estimates as well as detection of overfit. 

Each tree was fit using a DecisionTreeClassifier with:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Max\_depth = 15: to prevent unnecessarily deep trees that could memorize the training data instead of learning the patterns.
    \item Min\_samples\_leaf = 1 percent of training sample size: This prevents the tree from creating trees that are too small or specific, limiting overfitting
\end{itemize}
To illustrate the instability of single trees, we trained two trees on different samples of the training data. However, the same test set was used to evaluate the performance of each tree. Since diabetes status is a binary outcome, the model was evaluated using classification-based metrics rather than regressors like R2. Overall this approach to use multiple trees demonstrates that fundamentally decision tree models have high variance. Therefore for each tree we evaluated:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Accuracy which provides an overall measure of correctness
    \item True Positive Rate (TPR) which describes the proportion of diabetic respondents correctly predicted as diabetic
    \item True Negative Rate (TNR) which describes the proportion of non-diabetic respondents correctly predicted as non-diabetic
    \item Confusion matrices which provide the raw counts that underlie the rates. This allows us to see the absolute number of each error type.
\end{itemize}

   \label{alg:example}

\begin{definition}
\label{def:inj}
A function $f:X \to Y$ is injective if for any $x,y\in X$ different, $f(x)\ne f(y)$.
\end{definition}
Using \cref{def:inj} we immediate get the following result:
\begin{proposition}
If $f$ is injective mapping a set $X$ to another set $Y$, 
the cardinality of $Y$ is at least as large as that of $X$
\end{proposition}
\begin{proof} 
Left as an exercise to the reader. 
\end{proof}
\cref{lem:usefullemma} stated next will prove to be useful.
\begin{lemma}
\label{lem:usefullemma}
For any $f:X \to Y$ and $g:Y\to Z$ injective functions, $f \circ g$ is injective.
\end{lemma}
\begin{theorem}
\label{thm:bigtheorem}
If $f:X\to Y$ is bijective, the cardinality of $X$ and $Y$ are the same.
\end{theorem}
An easy corollary of \cref{thm:bigtheorem} is the following:
\begin{corollary}
If $f:X\to Y$ is bijective, 
the cardinality of $X$ is at least as large as that of $Y$.
\end{corollary}
\begin{assumption}
The set $X$ is finite.
\label{ass:xfinite}
\end{assumption}
\begin{remark}
According to some, it is only the finite case (cf. \cref{ass:xfinite}) that is interesting.
\end{remark}

\subsection{Citations and References}

Please use APA reference format regardless of your formatter
or word processor. If you rely on the \LaTeX\/ bibliographic
facility, use \texttt{natbib.sty} and \texttt{icml2025.bst}
included in the style-file package to obtain this format.

Citations within the text should include the authors' last names and
year. If the authors' names are included in the sentence, place only
the year in parentheses, for example when referencing Arthur Samuel's
pioneering work \yrcite{Samuel59}. Otherwise place the entire
reference in parentheses with the authors and year separated by a
comma \cite{Samuel59}. List multiple references separated by
semicolons \cite{kearns89,Samuel59,mitchell80}. Use the `et~al.'
construct only for citations with three or more authors or after
listing all authors to a publication in an earlier reference \cite{MachineLearningI}.

Authors should cite their own work in the third person
in the initial version of their paper submitted for blind review.
Please refer to \cref{author info} for detailed instructions on how to
cite your own papers.

Use an unnumbered first-level section heading for the references, and use a
hanging indent style, with the first line of the reference flush against the
left margin and subsequent lines indented by 10 points. The references at the
end of this document give examples for journal articles \cite{Samuel59},
conference publications \cite{langley00}, book chapters \cite{Newell81}, books
\cite{DudaHart2nd}, edited volumes \cite{MachineLearningI}, technical reports
\cite{mitchell80}, and dissertations \cite{kearns89}.

Alphabetize references by the surnames of the first authors, with
single author entries preceding multiple author entries. Order
references for the same authors by year of publication, with the
earliest first. Make sure that each reference includes all relevant
information (e.g., page numbers).

Please put some effort into making references complete, presentable, and
consistent, e.g. use the actual current name of authors.
If using bibtex, please protect capital letters of names and
abbreviations in titles, for example, use \{B\}ayesian or \{L\}ipschitz
in your .bib file.

\section*{Software and Data}

If a paper is accepted, we strongly encourage the publication of software and data with the
camera-ready version of the paper whenever appropriate. This can be
done by including a URL in the camera-ready copy. However, \textbf{do not}
include URLs that reveal your institution or identity in your
submission for review. Instead, provide an anonymous URL or upload
the material as ``Supplementary Material'' into the OpenReview reviewing
system. Note that reviewers are not required to look at this material
when writing their review.

% Acknowledgements should only appear in the accepted version.
\section*{Acknowledgements}

\textbf{Do not} include acknowledgements in the initial version of
the paper submitted for blind review.

If a paper is accepted, the final camera-ready version can (and
usually should) include acknowledgements.  Such acknowledgements
should be placed at the end of the section, in an unnumbered section
that does not count towards the paper page limit. Typically, this will 
include thanks to reviewers who gave useful comments, to colleagues 
who contributed to the ideas, and to funding agencies and corporate 
sponsors that provided financial support.

\section*{Impact Statement}

Authors are \textbf{required} to include a statement of the potential 
broader impact of their work, including its ethical aspects and future 
societal consequences. This statement should be in an unnumbered 
section at the end of the paper (co-located with Acknowledgements -- 
the two may appear in either order, but both must be before References), 
and does not count toward the paper page limit. In many cases, where 
the ethical impacts and expected societal implications are those that 
are well established when advancing the field of Machine Learning, 
substantial discussion is not required, and a simple statement such 
as the following will suffice:

``This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of 
Machine Learning. There are many potential societal consequences 
of our work, none which we feel must be specifically highlighted here.''

The above statement can be used verbatim in such cases, but we 
encourage authors to think about whether there is content which does 
warrant further discussion, as this statement will be apparent if the 
paper is later flagged for ethics review.
